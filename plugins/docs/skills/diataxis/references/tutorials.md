# Tutorials (learning-oriented)

A tutorial is a **lesson**. It takes a beginner by the hand and leads them, step by
step, to a successful, meaningful outcome. Its purpose is *learning* — to give the
newcomer confidence and a feel for the product — not to complete real work and not
to be comprehensive.

Position on the compass: **action + acquisition**. Practical (the learner *does*
things) in service of *study* (acquiring skill).

## The defining responsibility

**The teacher is responsible for the student's success.** If the learner follows
your steps and something breaks, that is the tutorial's fault, not theirs. A
tutorial must be reliable to the letter: every command works, every output matches,
on a clean environment. Test it end to end, repeatedly, and pin versions.

## What a good tutorial does

- **Delivers a visible, early, and repeated sense of achievement.** The learner
  should *do* something real and see it work quickly, then again and again.
- **Provides a concrete, meaningful result** — build a small but real thing, not a
  contrived "foo/bar" toy that teaches nothing transferable.
- **Shows, doesn't explain.** Minimise the *why*. A learner cooking their first meal
  doesn't need food chemistry. Point to explanation for the curious, but keep the
  flow moving.
- **Gets the learner doing immediately**, and keeps them in a state of "I did that,
  and it worked".
- **Is concrete, not abstract** — specific steps, specific inputs, specific outputs.

## What a tutorial must NOT do

- Don't explain concepts at length (that's explanation) — a sentence of context is
  fine; a lecture is not.
- Don't offer alternatives, options, or "you could also…" — the learner needs one
  known-good path, not decisions. Hide complexity.
- Don't be comprehensive — cover only what's needed to reach the result.
- Don't reference every possibility or edge case.
- Don't leave the learner to figure out steps themselves.

## Writing checklist

- [ ] Get the learner to a working result **fast**, then build on it.
- [ ] Every step produces a visible change; describe the **expected output** so they
      can confirm they're on track.
- [ ] Use the imperative and the first person plural where natural: "We will build…",
      "Now run…". Tell them exactly what to do.
- [ ] Assume **nothing** beyond the stated prerequisites; state those up front.
- [ ] One path, no branches. If a choice is unavoidable, make it *for* them.
- [ ] Point out what they'll see and why it matters — "notice that…".
- [ ] End with a clear sense of arrival and a pointer to what to do next (how-tos,
      explanation).
- [ ] Run the whole thing on a clean machine before shipping; keep it working.

## Smell test

If you find yourself writing "this is because…", "alternatively…", "for a full list
of options see…", or "in production you'd normally…", you've drifted out of tutorial
mode. Cut it or link out.
